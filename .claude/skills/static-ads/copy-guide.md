# Copy guide for static ads

Owns what the words say, for both `static-ads` and `offer-ads` (offer-specific rules in
`offer-ads/offer-copy.md`). Our own guide, built on Alex Cooper's statics guide and webinar,
Fraser Cottrell's statics guide (2026),
Parker's headline method (`.claude/skills/headlines/strategy.md`, once that skill is imported) and AI-tells check (`brain-system/creative-strategy-context/ai-writing-tells.md`), and our test runs. The copy itself is written in
the brand's language. Grow this file with real winners: when a brand has statics that work, analyze
them and add what repeats.

## Ground rules
- The headline is the hook. It has to land a feeling in one second. It's about the product, the
  problem or the result, in words everyone in the category gets ("Your phone's perfect spot on the
  cart."), never the season or the sale ("BLACK FRIDAY"): that goes in a small tag
- Customer words beat ours. Use them verbatim, even when the grammar is off: that's what makes it
  sound like a customer talking to a customer. Give the source in the copy table
- Top of funnel: assume nobody knows or cares about the brand yet. Lead with the problem, the
  situation or the result. Exception: ads for people who already know the brand. That's offers
  (`offer-ads`), and evergreen reminders with no offer, like a review plus a named product
  (awareness "knows the product" in decision 2; `testimonial/sw-SW004`)
- Short, but enough. Headline under about 10 words, but image and text together must tell a stranger
  what it is, who it's for and why it matters. If a line is cut so short it lost its subject
  ("Improvement I can see"), put it back
- Clear beats clever. If an 11-year-old wouldn't get it on first read, rewrite
- The CTA names the product, and the variant when there is one ("Shop pineapple coconut", not "Shop
  now"): people may not know it exists. Better still, it speaks the category's language and names
  what the product solves ("Get your phone a caddy →" for a golf-cart phone holder). "Shop now", "Buy
  now" and "Learn more" are out: Meta's button already says them (the lead strategist, 6 Oct 2026). Ask the brand which variant or SKU is rising, and build the
  ad around that one; when unsure, test the same ad across two or three SKUs. With no offer in it,
  the ad stays evergreen as long as the product is in stock (Fraser Cottrell, 2026)

## Decisions before writing (in this order)
0. **The offer brief.** If the brain has one (`strategy/offer-brief.md`), restate its big idea, the two mechanisms and the narrative angle at the top
   of the copy table. Every headline traces back to one of them or to a belief in its chain; write the
   link in the table's source column. A headline that traces to none is cut, or flagged "off-brief: the
   brief may need a new angle" for the human, never kept quietly to fill the batch. No brief: say so in
   one line and carry on; suggest writing one before the next batch
1. **Brand type for this product.** Problem/solution: the headline makes the customer recognize her
   problem and believe the fix. Lifestyle: the headline signals identity and makes the right person
   think "this is for me"
2. **Awareness.** Unaware of the problem: curiosity and education, no solution claims. Problem-aware:
   name the problem clearly. Tried other solutions: acknowledge they failed and show the difference.
   Knows the product: proof, offer, direct call. The colder the audience, the less product in the headline
3. **Market sophistication and experience (problem/solution).** How worn out the category's claims
   are (`brand-lens.md`, "Market sophistication") sets how much a plain claim can still do. Then the
   person. Tried a lot and skeptical: show you know the journey (myths,
   mechanism, "5 years of …"). New to it: keep it simple and clear
4. **Emotional depth (problem/solution).** Level 1 is the problem itself ("sore feet"). Level 2 is
   what she can't do because of it ("I've stopped the walks"). Level 3 is what it does to how she sees
   herself. Levels 2 and 3 beat level 1
5. **One persona per ad**, from the brain's personas. The others get their own ads. The strongest
   version has a landing page for the same persona; mention it when a persona gets several winners
6. **Clear or curious, not in between.** Either the ad sells on its own (clear what, who, why), or
   it opens a question and the page after the click sells. Then it needs a landing page that
   finishes the sale. A third place: the caption, when the image only stops the scroll and the
   primary text sells ("Native storytelling" below). The direction goes in the copy table

## Where the headline comes from (idea paths first, patterns second)
Pick the source before the structure. A headline built on something real beats a clever structure
built on air.
- **The brand's own winner:** reuse a structure that already works, with new content. First choice when one exists.
  Where to find it without account access: the brand's live ads in the Meta Ad Library (the brand's
  page, sorted by impressions). A text the brand runs in many ads, especially a customer quote it has
  chosen to run, is both verbatim and approved by the brand ("puts my phone in the perfect spot on the
  cart" became the headline the lead strategist kept, the golf test brand, 6 Oct 2026). Impressions are reach, not results:
  label it **Inferred (runs widely)**, never "proven"
- **A review, verbatim:** a customer line no strategist could have invented
- **Answer a comment:** name the objection in the customer's words and resolve it
- **The competitor's gap:** the promise a competitor can't make, with proof
- **A real number:** reviews, customers, years. Real numbers only
- **The template's headline**, translated to the brand's situation (same function, same length, own words)

If no idea path gives the line, pick a pattern. Both brand types: rejection ("Forget …"), metaphor,
strong verbs. Lifestyle: identity, status, "everyone's talking about", reaction ("I nearly cried"),
convenience. Problem/solution: name the problem directly, question, before/after in time, outcome
promise, "tried everything", authority, alliteration, parallel rhythm (two short matched lines;
not "No X. No Y. Just Z.", which the AI check bans).
Both types, when the research carries it: call the audience out by name so the right person
self-selects ("Til dig, der skal være farmor til dåben"). Call out the situation or the problem, not
only the label: "Moms of toddlers" alone is weak, and Meta reads the words to decide who to show the
ad to, so a callout that doesn't fit the ad's real audience steers it wrong (Stated, "creative for
2026" course video, 30 Sep 2026). Name the fear the customer won't say out
loud, in their words ("a gift you're embarrassed to give"); say what competitors are too polite to
say; lead with the core desire underneath the benefit (status, belonging, safety, approval), with the
product as the way there.
Depth per pattern: `.claude/skills/headlines/processes/<pattern>.md` once that skill is imported (`REWRITE-QUEUE.md`); until then, judgment.

In a batch: several different structures, not five versions of one. At least one should differ from
what the brand runs today.

## Quoting verbatim
- Whole sentences can be cut without marking
- A cut mid-sentence is marked with […]
- Capitals for emphasis are fine; the words don't change
- Keep the customer's own punctuation
- Remove reviewer and staff names when the brain's rules say so. Write source and month instead
  (e.g. "Review on Trustpilot, October 2024", in the brand's language)
- A quote keeps its real source. A website review isn't dressed up as a social comment with platform
  UI (likes, "3w · Reply"), and no generated face is shown as the reviewer: use initials, or a real
  photo with consent

## Native storytelling: when the caption sells
For templates where the image has no text and the primary text does the selling (`native/sw-SW005`,
`native/hp-07`). The image stops the scroll; the caption closes. In the copy table the full primary
text goes where the headline usually is. Source: Fraser Cottrell's statics guide (2026), with our
guardrails.

- **The image:** the problem itself, so close that someone who has it recognises it at once (the
  skin, the mess, the moment). No text, no product. A real customer photo with written consent, or a
  generated image that doesn't pretend to be a customer's. Never a found image recreated "different
  enough": that's a copyright problem, and a fake if the caption calls it a customer's
- **The first line is the headline.** Only about the first 125 characters show above "See more"
  (check in the preview). That line pairs with the image and makes the right person tap: who's
  talking and why it matters, in one breath
- **Who's talking decides what's allowed.** Two honest voices: a real customer's story, verbatim or
  cut per "Quoting verbatim", with written permission; or the brand or founder in their own name
  ("A customer sent us this photo" only when one did). Never a first-person customer story we wrote:
  under Danish and EU law, posing as a consumer is a banned practice, not a grey zone
- **A shape that reads as true:** the situation before, in concrete detail; low expectations; a slow
  arc with a setback ("week one, nothing"); a result that stops short of a miracle ("not gone, but the
  calmest in years"); the everyday moment it mattered. Understatement is what makes it believable,
  and it keeps the claims small
- **A list for self-selection:** 5-8 short lines in the reader's words, taken from reviews, each one a
  situation ("rolling your sleeves down even when it's warm"). The right person counts their own
- **Describe, don't diagnose.** Meta rejects ads that assert or imply the reader has a condition or
  other personal attribute ("Do you have eczema?", "Your dry skin…"). Write "my elbows", "people who
  …", or the situation itself, never "you have". Check the list line by line
- **Claims:** a health effect in a customer's story is the brand's claim once the brand runs it (EU).
  Same `brand-lens.md` check as a headline
- **Flow:** long is fine if it reads easily. Short paragraphs, one idea each, line breaks for mobile,
  a soft CTA at the end in the same voice. Chiefing and LIX apply to the parts we wrote; the
  customer's verbatim parts are exempt

## Copy check (step 4, before the human sees the table)
Flag every headline that fails one of these:
- Does it say something to someone who's never heard of the brand? (Skip for ads aimed at people who
  know the product, see the ground rules)
- Is it instantly clear what it is, or what problem it solves?
- One persona and one specific feeling?
- The customer's language, not marketing language?
- **The testimonial trap:** praise of the product ("Best ever", "Love them") says nothing about the
  person. Exception: quotes that carry identity, a vivid image or real emotion
- Could it sit in any other brand's ad? Then it's too generic
- Does it match what the image shows? If the headline says "comfort", the image shows comfort
- Does it sound like the brand's own ads? Read it next to three of their real ones
- **So what?** A question, open loop or teased tension is only half a message: it has to be paid off
  in the ad itself (subline, image, small text). Exception: the deliberately curious direction,
  where the page after the click pays it off
- **Brand + plain category at the first mention** for cold audiences ("Acme-proteinbaren", not
  "Focus"), in the headline or the line under it. A sub-brand or model name alone means nothing
  to a stranger
- **One writing style per ad.** If the headline is a confession, the subline carries on confessing;
  it doesn't switch to a sales pitch

## Chiefing (also step 4): read it the way it will be read
The copy check asks whether each line is right; chiefing asks whether the whole text flows. Copy
should read like a greased slide: nothing that makes the reader stop, reread or feel a company
talking at them.

- **Read every text as spoken**, line by line, and flag the bumps: a sentence over about 15 words,
  a long compound noun that's hard to say ("kundetilfredshedsgaranti"), two ideas in one sentence,
  passive or company voice ("vi tilbyder", "der ydes"), a word no customer would say out loud
- **LIX on body text of 30 words or more** (the primary text above the ad, a long letter or advertorial):
  run `python ".claude/skills/static-ads/lix.py" "<text>"`. Never estimate it.
  Aim for under 30; flag anything over 35, with the long words the script lists as the first
  place to cut. Product, brand and place names count as long words and inflate the score ("Nordahl
  Andersen", "Rødekro"): skip them in the list, and never cut a name to lower the number. Headlines and short image text are too short for LIX: read-aloud only
- **Customer quotes are exempt** from both, like in the AI check: they ship verbatim
- **In the table:** the LIX number next to each body text, and the bumps as fixes already made or
  flagged. Under the table, one line for the human: read the approved copy out loud once before
  saying yes; where you stumble, the reader will too

## AI check (also step 4)
Generated copy has recognizable patterns. Density matters, not a single word. The signs:
- Inflated words and brochure language: "elevate", "transform", "unique", "groundbreaking", "seamless", "nestled"
- Symmetry: "not just X, it's Y", neat triads of equal weight ("style, comfort and quality")
- Vague attribution: "experts say", "customers love" with no source
- Rhetorical self-questions answered at once ("The result?") and bridge phrases ("The best part?")
- Throat-clearing openers ("When it comes to …") and synonym cycling (the item, the product, the piece …)
- Formatting: lots of dashes, caps or bold that the brand doesn't use itself
- Hard bans, whatever the swipe example does (Alex Cooper's kill list, 2026): no em dashes (–, —) in ad
  copy; "Not X. It's Y." in any form ("Det er ikke X. Det er Y."); "No X. No Y. Just Z."
  ("Ingen X. Ingen Y. Bare Z.": say what the product is instead); "That's it." / "Sådan." as a
  closing beat; "say goodbye to … say hello to" ("Sig farvel til … sig goddag til") in one line.
  Customer quotes are exempt, as below

Fix by writing the line the way the brand's own ads or its customers would say it, not by swapping
one word for a synonym. **Customer quotes are exempt:** if a real review says "transformed", it
ships verbatim. The factory's lint script (`brain-system/scripts/voice-lint.py`) has an English word list, so for
other languages judgment carries the check. Detail: `brain-system/creative-strategy-context/ai-writing-tells.md`, sections "The sign families" and "The false-positive discipline".

## Persona panel (also step 4, after the two checks above)
A second opinion from the people the ads are for, before the human sees the table.

- **Who sits on it:** each persona in the brain's `personas/personas-profile.md` that has real evidence
  quotes (skip Data-limited ones: a persona built on guesses gives confident, empty scores), plus one
  strict reviewer who checks claims against `brand-lens.md` and the Danish marketing rules
- **How:** a subagent on Sonnet that didn't write the copy. It gets the personas with their quotes,
  the brand lens and the copy table, and nothing else. Each persona scores the ads made for it, 1-10:
  "would this stop me, and do I believe it?", with one line on why in that persona's own words. The
  reviewer answers pass / fail per ad
- **Only from the evidence:** each persona answers in the first person using only its card and
  quotes. When an ad turns on something the evidence doesn't cover (an objection, a situation, a
  feeling nobody quoted), it drops the character and returns "no data: <what's missing>" instead of
  a score. No data isn't a fail: it goes in the table as-is, and the gap is a lead for
  `running-notes/missing-context.md`
- **Threshold:** under 7 from its own persona, or a fail from the reviewer: rewrite once and score
  again. Still under 7: keep it, flagged, and let the human decide. Two rounds at most
- **In the table:** a score column and the one-line reason. Label it **Inferred (persona panel)**.
  It's a filter against weak drafts, not evidence: never quote the scores as if customers said them,
  and never let a high score outrank a real result in `brand-lens.md` ("what's worked")
- **Skip it** when the brain has no evidenced personas yet; say so in one line

## Meta text for the picks (step 9)
The ad also needs the text Meta shows around the image. Written only for the final picks, so no
effort goes on ads that get cut. Same voice, rules and checks as the image copy, from the same
customer language. Source: the Madvera image-ad skill (2026), cut down.
- **Primary text, two lengths:** short (about 50 words) and long (up to about 200, for testing).
  Only about the first 125 characters show before "See more", so the first line has to work alone.
  Native storytelling ads already have theirs
- **Headline** (under the image): 3-6 words, works as a call to action, names the product. It doesn't
  repeat the image's headline
- One version of each; the human asks for alternatives if needed
- Chiefing and LIX, the AI check and the claims check against `brand-lens.md`, as for the image copy

## Structured headline ideation (only for a new concept with no template)
One lead headline plus 2 variants in different patterns, three in total. For the lead strategist: one sentence
on why it stops the scroll. Show them before template and generation. Use this only here, not per
template in a normal batch: it costs too many tokens.

## Avoid
Claims the brand doesn't make itself, undocumented numbers, discounts that don't exist, and
headlines that only work on people who already know the brand.
