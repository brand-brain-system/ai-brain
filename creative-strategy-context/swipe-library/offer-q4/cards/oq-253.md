**Layout forensics.** The frame reads top to bottom as banner, photo, price. A full-bleed navy banner opens the ad with a two-line white claim set in heavy sans, all caps — the first fixed anchor of the Z path. Under it, the before/after split takes over roughly half the frame: two torso halves in one continuous cool-toned photo, "before" on the left showing loose skin and stretch marks under dark fabric, "after" on the right showing a smooth torso under pale fabric, with the product device floating dead-center over the seam where the two halves meet, so the product itself becomes the hinge the comparison turns on. Below the photo, the copy stack shifts to a plain white ground: a small black all-caps line, then a red heavy condensed headline set noticeably larger than anything else on the page — the true scroll-stop, not the top banner — then a full-width red offer banner carrying the code, then a plain black price anchor pair at the very bottom with no strike mark, just old and new price separated by an em dash. The eye lands first on the red headline because size and color contrast both favor it over the navy banner above; second on the before/after photo because it occupies the largest single area of the frame and the product bridges the two halves; third on the red offer banner, which repeats the red of the headline to keep the eye moving down into the deal.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe a body result that normally requires a clinic visit is achievable at home with this one device, and the before/after split is doing the entire persuasion job — the copy only has to label what the photo already proves. Image and words are congruent because the top banner's claim ("clinic-level, at home") is literally staged in the photo below it: the device sits at the exact point where "before" becomes "after," so the product reads as the mechanism of the transformation rather than an accessory to it.

**Copy skeleton.** Eyebrow: states the category claim the photo will prove, comparison mechanic, 5 words. Subhead: reframes the benefit as an emotional payoff rather than a physical one, command mechanic, 5 words. Headline: names the sale event in the fewest words at maximum size, announcement mechanic, 3 words. Offer: states the discount and the code together in one breath, number-plus-code mechanic, 6 words. Price: anchors old against new without a strike device, comparison mechanic, 4 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs a real before/after pair to work at all — it is the whole argument, and without it the product photo has nothing to hinge on. A brand without before/after proof would have to drop this template entirely rather than fake the split; there is no legal swap for a fabricated result photo. What does swap cleanly: the offer banner's mechanic (percent off, dollar off, free gift, bundle, free shipping, and promo code all fit the same red strip without touching the photo), and the top claim line, which can be rewritten for any comparison the brand can actually prove. The plain price-anchor line at the bottom can gain or lose a strike mark, a percentage badge, or drop out entirely for a brand that doesn't want to print two numbers.
