**Layout forensics.** The backdrop is a soft two-tone pink, a slightly darker triangular wedge of pink meeting a paler pink at an angle behind the products, like a stage set rather than a flat color field. At the very top, a thin-outlined header card holds a small caps eyebrow line beside a small x-mark icon, with a serif-and-script headline directly below it inside the same card, mixing navy and pink type. Cutting across the frame just under that card, two ribbon banners cross in an X pattern — one solid black, one hot pink — each carrying the same bold white numeral-led claim repeated multiple times along its length, spilling past the frame edges on both sides. Below the ribbons, the product grouping sits centered on the pink surface: the foil pouch stands tallest in the middle, flanked by a black tumbler on the left and a brushed-steel canister on the right, with a small frother and a scoop laid flat at the base of the group, all shot as a styled tabletop bundle rather than a clinical flatlay. Four of the accessory items carry small green flag-shaped "FREE" tags pinned at an angle, like price tags on a gift table. At the very bottom, a row of filled star icons sits beside a short bold customer-count claim. The eye lands first on the crossing ribbon banners (the only diagonal, highest-contrast black-and-pink element cutting through the whole frame), second on the product grouping (largest connected shape, centered, the pouch's saturated gradient pulling focus), third on the header card's script headline (smaller scale, top corner, read after the offer has already registered).

**The story this ad tells.** This wants the viewer to believe one purchase quietly turns into a small collection of useful things, not just a discounted bag of product. The crossing ribbon banners do the same job a flashing sign would in a store window, while the green flag tags on the accessories let the eye count the free items almost like unwrapping a gift table. The layout works for an offer because the value stack is shown, not just claimed — every "free" item is a real, separate object sitting on the table, which makes the bundle feel earned rather than inflated.

**Copy skeleton.** Eyebrow — a small superlative label inside the header card, 4 words. Headline — the sale name, split across a serif and a color shift, 3 words. Offer — a range-qualifier plus the discount numeral plus the mechanic word, repeated as a banner, 4 words per repeat. Badge — a single word per tag naming the mechanic, 4 words total across the four tags. Proof — a customer-count claim following the star row, 5 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout survives for any brand that can genuinely bundle three or four real accessory items around one hero SKU and back a customer-count claim with an honest number — swap the tumbler, canister, and frother for whatever accessories the brand actually offers as gifts, and the pouch for the brand's own hero packshot. A review count that can't be verified should become a guarantee seal instead, sitting in the same bottom-strip position, rather than an invented number. It breaks if the brand only has the one hero SKU with nothing to bundle around it — the whole centre zone depends on a real grouping of distinct objects, and a single product alone there would leave the "free gift" flags with nothing honest to attach to.
