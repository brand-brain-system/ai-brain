**Layout forensics.** The whole upper two-thirds of the frame is a single warm amber-gold surface lit from behind, like a plaster wall catching low light, and the sale copy is set directly into that surface as if it were carved or engraved rather than typed. The headline sits at the top in a loose handwritten script, two lines, the letterforms embossed in a darker brown so they read as texture on the wall rather than a graphic laid over it — that engraved quality is the whole visual hook, since nothing about it looks like a normal ad headline. Directly below, the offer line uses the same handwritten engraving but the discount figure is scaled up noticeably larger than the words around it, which is what pulls it into a giant-number read even though the whole block is stylistically one piece. At the very bottom edge of the frame, a row of product tops — five bottle or jar caps, evenly spaced — just begins to appear, cropped tight so only the tops are visible; the product is present but deliberately minor, more a signature at the bottom of the composition than a hero shot. There is no separate badge, banner, or button anywhere on the frame — the entire persuasive load sits inside the one engraved-text block. Eye order: the headline first, simply because it's the topmost and largest single word-group; the offer number second, since the enlarged percentage breaks the rhythm of the surrounding words and pulls the eye down to it; the product row last, functioning almost as a footnote that confirms there's something real being discounted.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to feel like they've stumbled on something etched permanently into a wall rather than a banner that will disappear tomorrow — the handwritten, engraved treatment borrows the credibility of something carved in stone to make a temporary discount feel weightier than a typical sale graphic. Image and words are basically the same object here: the "photo" is really a canvas for the type, and the product row at the bottom is the only thing anchoring the words to an actual purchase. It works for an offer ad because the format itself is the scroll-stopper — nobody expects sale copy to look hand-carved into a glowing wall, so the novelty buys the read before the discount number even lands.

**Copy skeleton.** Headline — announce the sale — event name — 3 words. Offer — name the discount and its scope — a percentage plus a reach claim — 4 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout runs on almost any brand whose product line can be shown as a row of similar-shaped units peeking up from the bottom edge — bottles, jars, tubes, boxes all work as long as there are several of them lined up. A single-SKU brand can still use it by showing multiple units of the one product instead of a lineup, which keeps the layout intact. The engraved-wall treatment needs no product visibility at all if the brand has no lineup to show — the product row can drop entirely and the ad still functions as a pure sale announcement, though it loses the implicit "this applies across the whole range" read. What breaks it: swapping the handwritten engraved type for a clean digital sans, which would strip away the entire reason this ad stands out from a normal sale graphic.
