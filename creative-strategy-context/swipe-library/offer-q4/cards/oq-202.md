**Layout forensics.** The whole frame is a dark wood tabletop shot from directly overhead, with folded sock pairs scattered loosely at every edge — no grid, no straight lines, deliberately casual, like a product just got pulled out of a drawer and tossed down for the photo. Each visible pair carries its own bold novelty print (a dinosaur, a lobster-and-crab scene, a monster face, a panda), so the border of the frame is doing the "look how many fun patterns we make" job without a single word of copy. Dead center, a plain white note card breaks the wood texture with a hard rectangle of negative space, and it's that contrast — flat white card against busy dark wood — that makes the center the obvious first stop despite having far less visual complexity than anything around it. On the card, three lines of loose black marker handwriting stack top to bottom, and the middle line is set noticeably larger than the other two, so within the handwritten block there's still a size hierarchy doing the same job a printed headline would. The handwritten, slightly imperfect letterforms and the card's faint drop shadow (it reads as physically laid on top of the tabletop, not composited flat) are the pattern-break device here — nothing else on the frame looks hand-made, so the one thing that does gets read as more personal and less like an ad. Eye order: the white card first (the one clean shape in a cluttered frame), the giant handwritten numeral within it second (it's the largest mark on the card), the surrounding sock patterns third, read almost as a photo backdrop rather than as competing copy.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe this is a spontaneous, low-production deal alert rather than a produced ad — the handwriting and the messy tabletop scatter both work against the polished-ad look on purpose, borrowing the visual register of a sticky note left on a desk. The product patterns doing the selling job around the edges (playful, giftable, a lot of variety) while the center card does the offer job (how much off) means the two halves of the frame never compete: the border sells the product category, the center sells the deal. It works for a percent-off promotion because the handwritten numeral makes the discount feel like a real, urgent decision someone just made and wrote down, not a permanent marketing claim.

**Copy skeleton.** Headline: the discount itself split across three lines for a stacked read, sale-name plus number, command-by-implication, 3 words total.

**Adaptation notes.** The layout is highly portable to any brand with a flatlay-able product in several distinct visual variants (patterns, colors, flavors) — the handwritten-note device doesn't care what sits around it, so a brand with a strong lineup of visually distinct SKUs can drop straight into this frame. It does not survive well for a single-SKU or visually uniform product line, since the border loses its "look how many options" job and the scatter starts to look empty rather than abundant. The handwritten note card itself is the one non-negotiable element — swapping it for printed type loses the entire "found note, not an ad" premise the layout is built on. A face or hands asset is never required here, which makes it one of the lower-asset-demand templates as long as the product photographs well loose and scattered.
