**Layout forensics.** The scene is a naturalistic indoor tabletop shot, soft window light coming from behind, wood surface running the full width of the frame. The product pouch stands upright and centered, angled slightly to show its face and a hint of its side, sharply in focus and the most saturated object in the shot against the warm neutral wood and pale wall behind it. Three separate yellow sticky notes are scattered around it, each carrying black handwriting: one leans against the pouch on the upper left with a per-day price breakdown, a second is stuck directly onto a glass drink tumbler to the right with a percentage claim and a small hand-drawn star, and a third sits lower in the frame, partly cropped by the bottom edge, carrying a short benefit line with its own star doodle. Five loose capsules are scattered on the table just in front of the pouch, a small realistic touch rather than a designed element. Nothing in the frame is a printed graphic — every piece of copy is a handwritten note, which is what gives this ad its improvised, someone-just-jotted-this-down quality. Eye order: the product pouch first, since it's the sharpest, most centered, most colorful object in the shot; the two upper sticky notes second, both competing for a similar second look since they sit at roughly the same height and both use the same high-contrast yellow-on-wood color; the lower cropped note third, smaller and partially cut off so it reads last; the loose capsules functioning as texture rather than a stop for the eye.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to feel like they're looking at someone's actual desk, mid-decision, with real handwritten reminders about price and value rather than a designed sales graphic — the sticky-note device borrows the credibility of a personal note to make the discount feel like a fact someone jotted down rather than a marketing claim. Image and words work together because the notes sit physically on or near the product they describe, so the price-per-day note anchors against the pouch and the percentage note anchors against the drink, tying each claim to something tangible in the frame. It works for an offer ad because the handwritten, cluttered-desk feel reads as native and low-pressure, which suits a brand making a daily-affordability argument rather than a loud blowout sale.

**Copy skeleton.** Offer (price note) — anchor the daily cost — a price-per-day breakdown — 4 words. Offer (discount note) — name the discount — a percentage claim — 2 words. Headline (benefit note) — name the payoff — a short benefit phrase — 2 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout works for any brand whose product can sit convincingly on a real tabletop with a couple of props nearby — it needs no face, no lifestyle scene, and no lineup, just one hero unit and a surface to set it on. The three sticky notes are individually swappable: the price-per-day note only works if the brand has a genuine low daily cost to anchor against, and without one it should become a straight percentage-off note instead rather than inventing a price claim. The star doodles are purely decorative and can be dropped or kept regardless of the copy. What breaks it: replacing the handwritten sticky notes with printed price tags or digital badges, which would erase the personal, unstaged quality the whole format depends on.
