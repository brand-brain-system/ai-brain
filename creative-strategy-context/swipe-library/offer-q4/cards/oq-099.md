**Layout forensics.** A flat mint-green field carries the whole frame; nothing photographic anchors it, so every other element has to earn its own weight. At the top, a two-word headline runs in heavy black condensed caps stacked on two lines — it is by far the largest type on the ad and takes up roughly a quarter of the vertical space, which is what makes it the obvious first stop for the eye. Immediately below, two diagonal yellow ribbon bands run corner to corner behind the product, each carrying the same short offer line in bold black caps; because the ribbons repeat and cross the product itself, they read as one loud offer plane rather than two separate messages. The product sits dead center: two units of the same toy in different colorways, tilted and crossed like an X, which gives the flat background some depth and turns two SKUs into one composition. A red circular starburst badge is tucked at the lower right of the product, white bold type stacked three lines deep — the only warm, saturated color on the page, so it fights the yellow ribbons for the second look even though it's smaller. Small black stars and dots are scattered loosely around the headline and product as pure decor, doing nothing but adding a bit of energetic texture. The bottom quarter is a solid dark-green banner strip running the full width, carrying a two-line benefit statement in white bold sans — a fixed anchor that closes the frame the way a caption closes a photo. Eye order: the giant headline first (size alone wins it), the crossed product second (it's the only three-dimensional, colorful thing on an otherwise flat field), then the red badge third (the only warm accent color, so it pulls attention out of turn), with the ribbon offer and the bottom banner read last as supporting text.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe a seasonal deal this big, on a toy this good, will not wait — the flat color field and the crossed dual-color product keep the whole thing feeling like a poster rather than a photo, so the size of "BLACK FRIDAY" can do the selling instead of a lifestyle scene. The words and the image agree with each other completely: the headline announces the sale, the ribbons and badge state the mechanics, and the product just sits in the middle proving there's something real to buy. It works for an offer ad because there's only one thing to look at and only one thing to decide — click or don't — and the layout never asks the eye to choose between two competing entry points.

**Copy skeleton.** Headline — announce the sale — one urgent two-word phrase — 2 words. Offer (ribbon) — name the discount mechanic — a percentage callout — 4 words. Badge — name a second mechanic and remove risk of missing it — a short gift/bonus callout — 5 words. Subhead (bottom banner) — tell them why the product matters day to day — a benefit claim — 8 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout survives almost any product that photographs well as two crossed units — a two-color variant pair, a bundle of two sizes, or two flavors of the same SKU all keep the composition intact. A single-SKU brand can still run it by crossing two identical units instead of two colorways, or by dropping to one unit centered and losing the "crossed" energy, which is a legal simplification, not a break. The red starburst badge can carry any short claim — free gift, bonus item, or a guarantee seal if there's no real gift to offer — without disturbing the layout. The ribbon offer needs an actual percent-off or dollar-off number; if the brand has no live discount, this becomes a "buy more, save more" ladder instead and the ribbon text swaps accordingly. What breaks it: adding a face or lifestyle scene behind the product, which would fight the flat poster read this layout depends on.
