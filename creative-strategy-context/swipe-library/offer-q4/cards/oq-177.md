**Layout forensics.** Everything here is drawn, not photographed, and the whole point is that it looks unfinished. Two lines of black marker-script handwriting sit upper-frame, tilted just enough to read as casual rather than sloppy — that's the first read, both because it's the largest single block of type and because handwriting itself is a pattern-break against a feed of polished ads. Loose color emoji scatter around it with no grid logic, reinforcing the DIY feel. Center frame, a second handwritten block stacks three lines of decreasing formality — a price, a label, and a plea — the price numeral set noticeably bigger than the words around it, which makes it the second read even though the whole block is handwritten. Below and around that sits the "product": a line-art doodle of a howling wolf with an arrow pointing to a hand-drawn beaded bracelet, plus a small handwritten label naming which animal a buyer would be tracking. A small brand logo anchors the bottom, understated on purpose so it doesn't fight the joke. There's no offer device in the structured sense — no badge, no banner — because the whole ad is styled as a sticky note, and adding a polished device would undercut the bit.

**The story this ad tells.** This wants the viewer to believe the brand is in on the joke about ad fatigue and is charging them less for it — the self-deprecating headline about the designer being on leave gives permission to laugh, and the reward for laughing is a very low, very legible price. The hand-drawn wolf and bracelet still do the congruency job a real photo would: they show what's being sold and tie it to the "track a real animal" mechanic, just in a register that matches the joke instead of breaking it with a polished product shot. It works as an offer ad because the low-fidelity style is itself the scroll-stop, not a weakness the copy has to work around.

**Copy skeleton.** Headline: sets up the self-deprecating joke, two-line command mechanic, 10 words. Offer: names the price and the ask, number-plus-command mechanic, 6 words. Product label: names the specific animal being tracked, label mechanic (counted inside the product notes). Logo: brand mark, no persuasive copy.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs almost no real photography — just a willingness to run genuinely rough hand-drawn or doodled art and a logo. It only works for a brand whose voice can carry self-deprecation without undercutting trust; a brand in a serious health or financial category should not borrow this format as-is. The animal-tracking mechanic is specific to Fahlo's product; a different brand swaps the doodle subject for whatever its product actually does, but the "arrow pointing from a joke to the thing being sold" structure survives the swap. The price-plus-plea offer block can carry any simple mechanic — percent off, dollar off, a free gift — with copy changes only, but it cannot support a code box or a value list without breaking the loose, sketchy feel that makes this format work.
