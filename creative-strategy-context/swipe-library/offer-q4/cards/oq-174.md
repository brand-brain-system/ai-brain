**Layout forensics.** Solid black gives every white and red element maximum contrast, so the frame reads fast even though it's carrying five separate zones. A small white eyebrow sits top-center, then two centered lines of huge bold white sans below it — the biggest text on the ad and the first thing the eye lands on. A red rounded-pill badge sits directly under that, restating the use-case in a tighter, punchier claim. Center frame, the two knee sleeves overlap at a bent-knee angle so the product reads as worn even without a leg in the shot. A solid red circle badge sits to the right of the product with a numeral set noticeably larger than the words around it — high color contrast against black makes this the second read, ahead of the product itself. Diagonal caution-tape ribbons in red cross the lower third behind and over the product, functioning as both decoration and a soft deadline cue, since tape reads as "close this out." Nothing here is a decorative split screen or a stacked composite — every element subordinates to the one white headline at top.

**The story this ad tells.** The ad wants the viewer to believe this is a serious, time-boxed price break on a product built for a specific, named pain problem. The white headline names the product and its use plainly, the red pill badge sharpens that into the actual complaint it solves, the caution tape borrows a "limited window" feeling from retail signage, and the red circle badge makes the size of the discount impossible to miss. Congruency holds because the product shown is exactly the product named in the headline, worn at the angle that matches the complaint (a bent knee), so there's no gap for the eye to stall on.

**Copy skeleton.** Eyebrow: announces the sale event, label mechanic, 3 words. Headline: names the product plainly, label mechanic, 3 words. Subhead: sharpens the use-case into a specific complaint, label mechanic, 3 words. Badge: anchors the discount size with urgency, number mechanic, 3 words. Decor: reinforces the sale window, label mechanic (no independent word count, part of the visual device).

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs a product that photographs in a worn or bent pose without needing a visible face or body — good for sleeves, braces, wraps, anything with a natural "in use" silhouette. A brand with only one color variant can drop product_count to 1 without losing the frame; the overlap of two colors is a nice-to-have, not load-bearing. The caution-tape decor is the one element that only works for a genuinely time-boxed event — a brand running an evergreen or subscription offer should drop the tape rather than force a fake deadline, since that's exactly the kind of invented urgency that breaks trust. The red circle badge can carry dollar off, a bundle count, or a free-gift callout with copy changes only.
