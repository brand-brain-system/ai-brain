**Layout forensics.** This is a real photo, not a designed layout laid over a background, which is the whole device: it borrows the credibility of an unstaged warehouse shot. The product spill on the right — dozens of individually bagged pouches in pink, tan, and mauve tumbling out of an open cardboard box toward the camera — takes up the most visual weight and the most color, so it reads first even though it sits opposite the type. On the left, a white paper sign taped to the box carries four lines of black marker handwriting, all caps, naming the brand and the sale; a small blue sticky note overlaps its bottom corner with the actual discount and shipping terms, also in marker. The warehouse itself — exposed ceiling beams, a second stacked box, bare concrete floor — fills the background without competing, since it's dim and unstyled next to the bright product. Nothing here is a decorative split screen: the sign and the box are two halves of one believable scene, not two ads sharing a frame.

**The story this ad tells.** This wants the viewer to believe the discount is real and slightly chaotic — a warehouse team taped up a sign because there was too much stock to move quietly, not because a designer built a sale graphic. The product spill is the money shot: volume and color communicate "there's a lot of this and it's going fast" without a word of copy. The handwritten sign and sticky note do double duty as both the offer and as proof of authenticity, since a printed banner would break the behind-the-scenes illusion the photo is selling.

**Copy skeleton.** Headline: names the brand and the sale event, label mechanic, 4 words. Offer: states the discount and the shipping perk, list mechanic, 4 words. Product: no copy, visual proof and the money shot. Background: environmental context only, no copy.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs a product that photographs convincingly in bulk — multiple units in a box, a pile, or a stack — and a brand willing to shoot (or convincingly stage) an unpolished warehouse or fulfillment scene. A single-SKU brand with only one color can still run this; the color variety in the spill is a bonus, not load-bearing. A brand without warehouse access can substitute a believable stockroom or garage shot, but a studio product shot on white breaks the authenticity the whole format depends on — that swap does not survive. The sticky-note offer can carry a bundle count, a dollar amount, or a subscription callout with copy changes only; it cannot carry a review count or star rating without adding a zone this photo doesn't have room for.
