**Layout forensics.** The frame is a tight crop on a model's torso, cut off above the chest and below the hip, wearing a matching pink sports bra and high-waisted legging set against a bright, softly blurred gym studio. There is no separate graphic offer device anywhere on the image — instead, the sale message is written in black marker directly on the model's bare midriff, two lines stacked, mimicking the look of a note scrawled on skin rather than a designed ad element. That skin-written text is the single loudest thing in the frame simply because it's the only high-contrast mark (dark ink on light skin) sitting in the exact center where the eye naturally lands on a cropped body shot. Below the photo, a separate white bar carries a short bold black headline, with one word picked out in a bright contrasting color — the only other deliberate color accent besides the pink of the outfit. There's no packshot, no badge, no proof element; the entire ad is built from a body, a garment, and two handwritten lines. Eye order: the marker writing on the skin first, since it's the highest-contrast, most unusual mark in the center of the frame; the body and garment second, since that's the visual mass the writing sits on top of; the bottom headline bar third, smaller and separated from the photo, closing the loop by naming what's being sold.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe this deal is so immediate someone wrote it on themselves rather than waiting for a graphic designer — the atypical placement of the offer text is the entire scroll-stop mechanism, borrowing the raw, unpolished feel of a dare or a note passed between friends. Image and words work together because the product being sold (leggings) is literally the garment on screen, and the handwritten claim sits right at the point on the body the product is meant to transform, so the eye connects the offer to the promise in one glance. It works for an offer ad in this category because the atypical text format is a genuine pattern interrupt against a feed full of standard product shots, and the body itself becomes the medium for the message instead of a floating graphic.

**Copy skeleton.** Offer (skin-written) — announce the sale and name the mechanic — event name plus a buy-more deal — 4 words. Headline (caption bar) — name the product category with an emotional hook word — a short noun phrase with one word emphasized — 3 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs a candid body shot with a clear, flat stretch of skin or a comparable surface (a bare arm, a stomach, even a product's own surface) where handwritten text can sit legibly, plus the product worn or held in the same frame. A brand without a willing model can adapt the device onto the product's own packaging or a plain surface instead of skin, keeping the "written by hand, not designed" feel while dropping the body reliance — this is a legal swap that keeps the layout's core mechanism intact. What breaks it: replacing the handwritten skin text with a printed sticker or badge, which turns a raw, personal-feeling device into a standard ad graphic and loses the entire reason this format stops the scroll.
