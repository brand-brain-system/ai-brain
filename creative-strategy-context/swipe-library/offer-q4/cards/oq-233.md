**Layout forensics.** The ad opens like a social feed post rather than an ad: a large bold black headline sits top-left in plain sans type, with a single hand-drawn red underline stroke beneath the last word, giving it a marked-up, personal feel rather than a designed banner. Directly below, a pale-grey rounded rectangle mimics a Facebook comment card exactly — a small circular profile photo inset at the left edge, a bold name in black directly beside it, then two to three lines of the comment itself in regular weight with one word capitalised for emphasis and a single emoji closing it out. Under the card, a tiny row of grey UI text runs the width of the card: a timestamp, "Like," "Reply," and a reaction count with small icons, all rendered small enough to read as authentic interface chrome rather than ad copy. Below the comment block, two product jars sit side by side on plain white, shot flat and evenly lit — one open from above showing the cream's texture, one closed and labeled so the brand and product name are legible. The bottom of the frame is a full-width mustard-yellow banner in bold black caps, centred, the one loud color note in an otherwise black-white-grey composition. The eye lands on the black headline first, both for size and because the red underline is the only bright accent in the top half. Second is the comment card, whose screenshot styling and the small face photo create a pattern the eye recognises as "someone else already said this," pulling it in for a closer read. Third is the product pairing, and fourth the yellow offer banner at the very bottom, which closes the ad rather than opening it.

**The story this ad tells.** This layout wants the viewer to believe a stranger's unprompted comment, not the brand's own claim — the entire middle of the ad is styled to look native to a social platform, complete with UI chrome most ads would never bother rendering. The product only appears after the testimonial has done its work, positioned as visual proof of what the commenter is talking about rather than as the hero of the ad. The yellow banner at the bottom is the only place the brand actually asks for the sale, kept deliberately separate and lower-key in tone (all caps, but on a banner rather than mimicked as headline copy) so it doesn't undercut the "this looks like real social proof" read above it. It works as a testimonial-led offer ad because the discount is treated as a footnote to the social proof, not the other way around.

**Copy skeleton.**
- headline: hook with a superlative claim, statement, 4 words
- attribution: name the commenter, name, 2 words
- quote: prove the claim in the commenter's own words, quote, 24 words
- ui_chrome: render authentic interface detail, timestamp and counts, 4 words
- offer: announce the bundle discount, number, 6 words

**Adaptation notes.** Caption alignment (Alex, 2026-09-16, board feedback on Honeylove #5): the caption text and its white rounded background are both centred, as on the reference; never mix centred and left-aligned lines in the caption. Centred is preferred; fully left-aligned also works. This layout requires a real, sourced customer comment or review with a real name and, ideally, a real profile photo — the comment-card device only works as social proof if the quote is genuine; a paraphrased or invented line here would be the single fastest way to break trust in this format, since the whole point is that it reads as unstaged. A brand without a usable face photo can drop the small circular inset and let the comment card run as a plain review-card screenshot instead, which survives the layout intact. The product pairing (open jar plus closed labeled jar) is a strong single-SKU device and adapts easily to any product that photographs well both open and closed; a multi-SKU brand could swap it for a simple product lineup without disrupting the hierarchy. The mustard banner's mechanic can flex between a percent-off, bundle, or free-gift close with copy changes only.
