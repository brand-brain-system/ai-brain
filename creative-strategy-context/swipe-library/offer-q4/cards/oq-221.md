**Layout forensics.** A full-width black bar sits at the very top with small-caps white type running edge to edge, the kind of banner that reads as a masthead before anything else loads. Directly under it, two stacked banner pills carry the offer: a black rounded rectangle with the discount numeral in heavy white sans, immediately followed by a red rounded rectangle underneath it in the same weight but black text on red, the two reading as one continuous "base discount, then extra discount" staircase. Both pills run almost the full frame width and are rotated very slightly, giving a hand-stamped, sticker-on-sticker feel rather than a flat graphic. Below that, two short lines of plain grey sans type sit left-aligned and un-decorated, quiet by comparison. The centre-to-lower two-thirds of the frame is the product zone: three wooden toy pieces are arranged in a loose vertical stack on a warm-grey tabletop, shot straight-on with flat, even lighting so every piece reads clearly at thumbnail size. At the very bottom, a single line of small black text separates three value-adds with bullet dots, sitting in open whitespace with room to breathe. The eye lands on the black-then-red offer pills first, because they're the largest, boldest, highest-contrast shapes in the frame and they're stacked to look like escalating value. Second is the product cluster, simply because it's the visual mass filling the centre of a 9:16 frame. Third is the black eyebrow bar, which reads early structurally but loses to the offer pills on sheer size. The bottom bullet line is last, functioning as a receipt line rather than a hook.

**The story this ad tells.** This layout wants the viewer to believe they're getting a rare stacked deal on a set they'd normally have to buy piece by piece — the two-tier pill treatment does the arithmetic for them before they've read a word of the subhead. The plain grey subhead lines exist only to name what's in the box after the discount has already landed the hook; the product shot proves the bundle is real and substantial by showing three distinct pieces rather than one. The bottom bullet line closes the loop with the incidentals (stock, free extra, warranty) that remove the last hesitations. It works for an offer ad because the discount is the whole story and everything else — copy, product, list — is sequenced to support that one number stack, never to compete with it.

**Copy skeleton.**
- eyebrow: announce the sale, command, 6 words
- offer (black pill): name the base discount, number, 2 words
- offer (red pill): name the stacked bonus discount, number, 2-word cue
- subhead: name the promo mechanism, short phrase, 2 words
- subhead: name the bundle, short phrase, 3 words
- list: remove risk and stack incidental value, list, 7 words

**Adaptation notes.** This layout is built for a brand with a genuine multi-piece bundle to photograph — a single-SKU brand can still run it by swapping the three-piece lineup for one product shot at three angles or three quantity tiers, which keeps the "staircase of value" read intact. The stacked black/red pill device survives almost any discount mechanic swap: percent off, dollar off, buy-X-get-Y, or a free-gift callout can all sit in the same two pills with copy changes only. The bottom bullet line is the flexible slot — swap "1-Year Warranty" for a guarantee seal, swap "Free Montessori eBook" for a subscription perk. What breaks the layout: adding a face or hands, since the composition depends on the product filling that centre mass cleanly against a neutral surface; a face would compete with the pills for attention and there's no zone built to hold one.
