**Layout forensics.** A single vertical gradient — dark navy at the top, warming to a soft peach at the base — carries the entire frame; there's no background photo or texture beyond the watermark, so every read here is about type scale, not imagery. A hairline wordmark opens the ad, small and centered, doing nothing but establishing whose sale this is. Below it, a thin all-caps eyebrow line names the sale window, then the frame's single dominant element: one word, enormous, set in a gradient fill that echoes the background's own navy-to-peach shift, so the headline reads as if it's made of the same light the ad is bathed in. That's the scroll-stop — size and a self-referential color treatment doing the work a photo would normally do. Directly beneath it, a subhead names the specific product, and under that the price anchor: the old number struck through in a lighter weight, the new number set bold and full-white right beside it, tight enough that the eye reads them as one comparison rather than two separate facts. The product sits below the price block, small relative to the frame, rendered as a floating hero shot — the earbuds pair hovering just above their case, angled to show both buds and the case's front edge. Behind the product, a repeating ghost-watermark of a product-descriptor phrase tiles across the frame at low contrast, texture rather than message. Below the product the canvas simply runs out into flat gradient with nothing in it — close to half the frame is empty. Eye order: the giant single-word headline first, the struck-through price second (it's the only other high-contrast type block), the eyebrow/subhead third, the product last — the actual thing being sold is the smallest and lowest-priority element on its own ad.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe this is a limited markdown on a premium object, and it makes that case almost entirely through typography and math rather than lifestyle proof — no face, no scene, just the sale word, the price comparison, and the product floating in its own gradient light. The strikethrough does the persuasive work a testimonial or review count would do elsewhere: it's the "proof" that this is a real deal, made visible instantly without requiring the viewer to read a claim. It works for a price-anchor offer because the whole argument is a single subtraction the eye can do in under a second — old number, new number, done — and the empty lower half of the frame, while a real waste of space, doesn't hurt that argument since nothing needed to live there.

**Copy skeleton.** Logo: brand mark, wordmark only, 1 word. Eyebrow: names the sale window, date-range framing, 5 words. Headline: the sale itself, single noun, 1 word. Subhead: names the specific product being discounted, 4 words. Price: the comparison, before number and after number, command-by-implication, 2 words.

**Adaptation notes.** The layout survives without this exact product if the brand has any clean hero packshot that can float on a gradient — no face or lifestyle asset is required, which makes this one of the lowest-asset-demand templates in the set. It does not survive without a real, verifiable before/after price; if the brand can't show two honest numbers, the strikethrough zone has to become a straight percent-off badge or a giant-number treatment instead, since a fabricated "was" price is a fabricated claim. The empty lower half is free real estate — a brand with proof to add (a guarantee seal, a short spec list) can use it without disturbing the hierarchy above. Multiple SKUs would break the single-hero-object feel unless reduced to a tight grid in the same product zone.
