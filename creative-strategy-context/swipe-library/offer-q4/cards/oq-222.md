**Layout forensics.** The frame opens on a solid deep-maroon field with a small centred wordmark near the top — quiet, functional, not competing for attention. Directly below it, a short line of small white sans ("UP TO") sits beside a huge italic numeral set at roughly triple the height of anything else on the page, the classic giant-number device doing the scroll-stopping work on its own. Under the number, a multi-line block of bold white caps runs centred across most of the frame width, mixing upright and italic emphasis on select words — this is a lifted customer quote dressed up as a headline, large enough to be mistaken for one at a glance. A small caps attribution line sits directly beneath it, then a white rounded pill button breaks the maroon field with a hard color-contrast CTA. The bottom third shifts from flat color to an actual photograph: four grooming products are lined up on a tabletop toned to match the maroon above, shot straight-on and evenly lit, with a single Christmas ornament cropped in at the frame edge as a seasonal prop. The eye lands on the giant italic percentage first — it's the largest single mark on the page and it's white on maroon, the highest-contrast pairing available. Second is the quote block, which is nearly as large but reads as a sentence rather than a single glyph, so it takes a beat longer to land. Third is the product lineup, pulling the eye down into the photograph. The CTA button, though high-contrast, is small and reads last, as a closer rather than a hook.

**The story this ad tells.** The layout borrows credibility from a real customer before it ever shows the product: the discount hooks first, then a testimonial does the emotional selling ("best gift I've ever given him") before a single item has been seen, and only then does the actual lineup appear to prove the claim is about a real, tangible set of products. Image and words are sequenced rather than layered — solid color and giant type up top, real photography down below — so the ad reads like a poster that resolves into a catalog page. It works for a holiday-gifting offer because the emotional case (this is a great gift) and the rational case (here's what's in the box, and it's discounted) are handled by two different visual registers instead of fighting for the same space.

**Copy skeleton.**
- offer: announce the discount, number, 4 words
- quote: prove the gift-worthiness, quote, 19 words
- attribution: source the quote, name, 2 words
- cta: tell them what to do, command, 2 words

**Adaptation notes.** This layout needs a real, sourced customer quote to run as designed — the quote is doing more selling than the product shot, so a brand without a usable testimonial should either pull a real one verbatim or drop the quote zone entirely and let the giant number and product photo carry the ad alone; do not paraphrase or invent a line here. The maroon solid-to-photo transition survives almost any product category swap since it only needs a tabletop shot in a toned-to-match setting. The discount device (giant italic numeral) can carry percent off, dollar off, or a bundle price with copy changes only. What breaks the layout: swapping the quote for a review count or star rating changes the persuasive mechanic entirely and would need its own zone treatment, not a straight substitution.
