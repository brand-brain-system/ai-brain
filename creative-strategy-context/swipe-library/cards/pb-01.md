## Body

**Layout forensics.** The top half of the frame is a flat black field carrying a two-line, oversized bold white sans-serif headline in tight caps, running almost edge to edge — this is the first thing the eye lands on. Directly below it, a shorter two-line subhead sits in a warm gold/tan weight, noticeably smaller and lighter, second in line. The bottom half of the frame drops the black field into a photographed wood-stump surface: the product jar sits centered-left on the wood, lit from above with a soft top-down key light, coarse sea salt scattered loose around its base and a small potted succulent placed just to its right for scale and warmth — third thing the eye catches, since it's the only organic color note against the dark type block. The brand's pine-tree logo mark sits small, bottom right, white on the dark background edge. Nothing else competes for attention; the composition is a clean stack of headline / subhead / product with no ornamentation.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe their current routine is under-serving them and this product is the upgrade — the headline makes the emotional claim ("you deserve better"), the subhead immediately backs it with concrete, sensorial proof points (ingredient, effect verbs), and the product shot closes the loop by showing exactly what "better" looks like, staged with natural props that signal quality and craft rather than mass-production. This layout carries a single strong emotional/aspirational hook best — it wants one killer line, not a list of features, and leans on photography (not badges or reviews) to do the trust-building.

**Copy skeleton.** headline — hook — statement — 4 words. subhead — prove/explain — statement — 9 words.

**Adaptation notes.** Needs one genuinely strong headline line (VOC-preferred, per the fit note) and a subhead that can carry 2–3 concrete benefit or ingredient claims without feeling like a list. The wood-stump-and-succulent staging is swappable for any on-brand surface (marble, linen, concrete) as long as it's photographed, not flat-colored — that keeps the layout's warmth intact; dropping to a plain solid backdrop breaks the "premium, considered" feel this template trades on. No people in frame, so no content-filter risk.
