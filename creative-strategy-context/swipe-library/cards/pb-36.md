## Body

**Layout forensics.** A real-looking kitchen sets the stage, background softly blurred — stainless appliances, cabinetry, daylight through a window. Center-back, a small dry-erase whiteboard is propped against the counter carrying two hand-drawn marker sketches side by side: a bloated torso in profile on the left labeled "Before...", an arrow, then a flatter torso on the right labeled "After!" — deliberately crude, like something sketched in ten seconds, not designed. Beneath the drawings, three lines of handwritten black-marker copy spell out the hook in plain, conversational language. In the foreground, a hand holds the product bottle at a slight angle just beside the whiteboard, label facing camera, close enough to the lens to feel casually placed rather than composed. Nothing here reads as ad production — no logo overlay, no clean typography, no studio lighting. Eye path: the whiteboard drawing first (it's the novel, illustrative element that breaks the pattern of a normal product photo), then the handwritten line beneath it, then the product held alongside.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe a real person sketched their own before/after on a kitchen whiteboard to explain why the product worked for them — the crude drawing does more persuasive work than a polished graphic would, because its imperfection reads as authenticity. Image and words split the job cleanly: the drawing carries the transformation claim visually, the handwritten line names the problem it solves, and the product held in-frame closes the loop by making it obvious which product gets credit. This layout carries a genuinely drawable before/after state best — categories where the change is visible and simple enough to sketch in two stick-figure-adjacent outlines (bloating, posture, coat condition), not ones that need photographic proof to be believable.

**Copy skeleton.** decor — hook: illustrate the transformation without a word; drawing, no copy. body — hook: name the problem in plain spoken language; statement; 9 words.

**Adaptation notes.** Needs a transformation the brand can honestly claim and one that reduces to a simple, sketchable before/after shape — this doesn't work for categories where the change is subtle or requires a real photo to be credible. Requires a real hand holding the actual product in frame; a stock or studio hand shot breaks the native illusion. Legal swap that keeps the layout intact: change the specific before/after states and the handwritten hook to match the brand's real use case, keeping the crude two-drawing format. Swap that breaks it: cleaning up the whiteboard art into something polished, or overstating the transformation beyond what the product can support — the drawing is still a transformation claim and needs the same scrutiny a photographic before/after would get. Flag for compliance review before running, since it is functionally a before/after claim even though it's illustrated rather than photographed.
