## Body

**Layout forensics.** Two real photographs are stitched side by side down a thin dashed white vertical line, mimicking a CapCut split edit. Spanning the seam at the top, a white rounded handwritten-style headline reads across both halves at once, with a small knife icon trailing it — this is the first read, since it's the only text that crosses the whole frame. Below it, each half carries its own two-line handwritten caption ("before"/"after" plus a matching emoji) — second read, immediately orienting the viewer to which side is which. The bottom two-thirds of each half is pure photography: the left panel is dim, under-cabinet-lit, showing a mangled, juice-soaked tomato beside a visibly worn knife on a stained cutting board; the right panel is bright, window-lit, showing a whole tomato, a clean sharp knife and neat tomato rounds, with the product itself sitting quietly on the counter in the background — third read, where the actual proof lives. Nothing is staged as a graphic element; every mark on the page reads as something a real person would type into a phone's caption tool.

**The story this ad tells.** It wants the viewer to believe a fast, low-effort action (two minutes with the product) produces an obviously better, visibly different result — the split-screen device does the persuasive work without a single claim being stated outright; the viewer draws the "before is bad, after is good" conclusion from the photography alone, and the casual handwritten captions keep it feeling like an unscripted phone post rather than a produced ad. This layout carries a plausible, visually obvious transformation best, and it works even without a person in frame, since the "before" and "after" states of the object itself (here, the tomato and the knife's edge) do the storytelling.

**Copy skeleton.** headline — hook — statement, spans the split — 4 words. subhead (before) — compare — statement — 2 words. subhead (after) — compare — statement — 2 words.

**Adaptation notes.** This reference demonstrates that the before/after device does not require a person — it uses a knife-sharpener-on-a-tomato demo instead of a body transformation, even though this template category is more commonly used for visible personal transformations (beauty, fitness, skin, coat/fur). For those categories, if the brand's version puts a real person's body or face in the before/after photos, flag it for content-filter review before generating (nudity/body-image policy risk); for an object- or result-based demo like this one, that risk doesn't apply. Either way, this layout is inherently claim-sensitive because it is a before/after — it needs human review regardless of category, to confirm the transformation shown is honest and not exaggerated by staging (e.g. deliberately messier "before" lighting or props). Build this at 4:5 — the reference image measures 4:5, not the 9:16 the source prompt states, and the reference content (a no-person kitchen demo) diverges substantially from the source prompt's mirror-selfie/bathroom scenario, so treat the prompt as a starting point only, not a literal spec.
