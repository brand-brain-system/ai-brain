## Layout forensics

Three horizontal bands stacked top to bottom. The top band is a plain white strip carrying a massive, bold two-word statement in dark charcoal uppercase, closed with a period for punch — it takes up roughly the top sixth of the frame and reads before anything else purely on scale and contrast. The middle band, the largest, is a soft-lit lifestyle photo: a hand dropping a single capsule above an open bottle on white marble, cap and loose capsules resting beside it, product label turned toward camera. The bottom band sits on a warm gold-tan field and holds three evenly spaced columns, each a thin black line-icon in a circle over a short bold black uppercase label — curb, boost, support, one icon-word pair per idea. A dark scrolling ticker bar runs along the very bottom edge, repeating a short social-proof line in small bold type. First landing is the giant top statement (biggest single mark, top of frame); second the lifestyle product photo (largest area); third the icon row, read left to right.

## The story this ad tells

It wants the viewer to believe the product does one specific job so well it deserves a blunt, almost combative one-line claim, then backs that claim with a calm, well-lit product shot and three quick supporting reasons. The statement does the stopping; the photo does the trusting; the icon row does the explaining. This layout carries a single sharp positioning claim best — it needs one product with one dominant use case, since the top statement leaves no room for nuance or a second idea.

## Copy skeleton

Headline (statement) — 2-3 word blunt positioning claim, ends on a period, 2 words.
List (explain) — three icon-plus-label benefit columns, 6 words total across 3 items.
Stat (prove) — social-proof ticker line, repeating, 5 words.

## Adaptation notes

Needs one product with a genuinely dominant, ownable use case — the blunt top-line statement collapses if it has to cover multiple unrelated benefits. The three icon benefits should be real product mechanisms, not just adjectives, since they're the only explanation the ad gives. The ticker line needs a real, verifiable count (customers, units sold, reviews) — an invented number in a scrolling social-proof bar is an easy compliance flag; if no real count exists, the honest swap is a plain tagline in the same ticker position. A hand is shown but no face, so content-filter risk stays low.
